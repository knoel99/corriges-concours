## Commentaire

### Keeping the family name in circulation

John Wheelwright opens with a claim about what counted in his town. One might expect such a claim to settle who he is before the story of Owen Meany begins. Instead, one name starts a chain of stories about other names, until the reader is somewhere near the mouth of a New Hampshire river in the seventeenth century. Why should it take so long to introduce oneself? In John Irving’s novel a name gives its bearer a place among other people, but it also circulates without its bearer’s permission. The storyteller derives much of his authority, and a good deal of his pleasure, from following its decidedly improper travels.

John first places himself inside a plural. A Wheelwright commands a degree of respect which the people called Meany evidently do not. The repetition of his surname sounds like a child learning the local order of precedence. Yet the formidable representative of the line is a woman who acquired the name by marriage. The death of her young husband put her in charge. Her own maiden name and her descent from John Adams give her two further claims on public attention. The comedy depends on the exact distribution of these assets. What counts in this particular town is her husband's name, but what makes that name count is the assurance with which she uses it. One need not travel back to the Pilgrims to see a family tradition being made. The reader can watch Harriet perform it in the space of a paragraph.

No comparable courtesy attends her mental picture of a Meany. A labour leader with a cigar supplies it perfectly, although the narrator knows of no family connection. The parenthesis clears the local Meanys of a wholly imaginary association while letting us enjoy Harriet’s image for a few seconds longer. It also makes the narrator’s practice uncomfortably like hers. How readily he reduces her view of class to a pairing of unions and cigars! His qualifications, conjectures and corrections can express scruple, yet they are also excellent ways of lodging a picture in a reader's mind. Harriet would have considered it rather important who had the right to a name. The grandson who tells this tale knows what a storyteller can do with a resemblance.

The dog called Sagamore gives the joke historical depth. A term applied to Indigenous leaders appears successively as the name of a lake, the name of a neighbour’s dog and a child’s mistaken word for a lake. The owners of each explanation can tell a perfectly plausible little story. Mr Fish did swim in the lake. John did grow up knowing the dog. The trouble starts when an incident within living memory is allowed to stand for an origin. The child learns the history of New England, as it were, from the wrong end. For an instant the adult turns to us with a promise. “As you shall see, it did.” He can guarantee the coming offence because he knows what happens to the dog. Quite how that death proves anything about offended gods remains open. The promise of certainty makes the shift from event to interpretation all the funnier. The mistake has political force. The ancestors of the settlers can be followed through names, colleges, churches and books. Authority on the other side of the purchase survives in a word whose history has been almost erased by its continued use. Even the grown narrator falls back on an expansive modern colloquialism about chiefs and other important people. Recovering a derivation has not recovered the people who bore the title.

Then the dog is run over by a diaper truck. The unceremonious collision of words puts a severe strain on any reader looking for providence in American history. John can give the waters an offended divinity, but he can think of a more satisfactory ending to his story than the divinities have thought of to theirs. Mr Fish, the man who did the naming, might have been the victim. The chagrin of a thwarted storyteller opens into a real difficulty of belief, the suffering inflicted on the innocent. The parenthetical roll call of friends who disagree manages to make theology sound intensely local again. After the dog's death, John's willingness to entertain a better story becomes troubling as well as funny. Imagine how much moral tidiness could be obtained simply by choosing who gets hit. Irving lets us hear the attraction of such an arrangement at the precise moment when events decline to oblige.

The family history returns with dates, places and an ancestor who actually bears the narrator’s Christian name. But the Reverend Wheelwright’s association with Cromwell produces testimony about tripping opponents at football. The great man's esteem for a Cambridge contemporary turns out to contain quite a bit of apprehension. Nor does the name Gravesend lead straight to an origin. The narrator reports an absence of known contacts with the English town before imagining a friend who failed to emigrate or died at sea. Variations in grammatical certainty matter as much here as dates. A life no historian can certify has slipped into the paragraph as a possibility. The private motive John invents for a public name tells us something about his own concern with absent friends. It does not entitle us to draw another branch of the family tree.

Finally, an identified local history supplies phrases used of the minister and the disturbances he allegedly caused. John quotes it, answers it and finds in his ancestor's obstinacy a precedent for his own religious doubts. English ecclesiastical discipline is followed, across the ocean, by the independent severity of the Puritans. Crossing the Atlantic has not taken the dissenting minister beyond the reach of authorities offended by dissent. And so the landscape towards which he sails is given to us half through scholarship and half through imagination. There are old and new place names, a forest whose density can only be inferred and people who, the narrator supposes, would have shown him the fishing. The fragment ends with another quotation from the local historian. Quotation has an honourable place in this form of memory, but the shifts leading up to it have made any final, all-knowing picture impossible. The coincidence of John’s name with his ancestor’s lends a vertical order to the history. Yet the exchange of fresh and salt water at the end offers another way of picturing it. Influences meet, and a family cannot claim to own the whole current. Even the fishing in John’s imagined colonial landscape depends on knowledge the newcomers had to receive. The name John inherits tells us who is speaking. The liberties he takes with that inheritance let us hear the particular, disputatious believer who speaks.

## Version française

Aux yeux d’Oliver Cromwell, Wheelwright était un joueur brutal, voire déloyal, qui était passé maître dans l’art de faire trébucher ses adversaires puis de leur tomber dessus. Gravesend, la ville anglaise, se trouve dans le Kent, assez loin des parages que fréquentait Wheelwright. Peut-être avait-il un ami originaire de là-bas. Peut-être était-ce un ami qui avait voulu faire le voyage en Amérique avec Wheelwright, mais qui n’avait pas pu quitter l’Angleterre ou qui était mort pendant la traversée.

D’après l’*Histoire de Gravesend (New Hampshire)*, de Wall, le révérend John Wheelwright avait été un bon pasteur de l’Église d’Angleterre jusqu’au jour où il s’était mis à « contester l’autorité de certains dogmes ». Il devint puritain et se vit par la suite « imposer le silence par les autorités ecclésiastiques pour cause de non-conformisme ». Mes incertitudes en matière de religion, et mon entêtement, doivent beaucoup, me semble-t-il, à cet ancêtre. Non seulement il essuya les critiques de l’Église d’Angleterre avant son départ pour le Nouveau Monde, mais à peine arrivé, il s’attira aussi à Boston les foudres de ses coreligionnaires puritains. Tout comme la célèbre Mme Hutchinson, le révérend Wheelwright fut banni de la colonie de la baie du Massachusetts pour avoir troublé « la paix civile ». À vrai dire, son acte le plus séditieux avait été d’émettre quelques opinions peu orthodoxes sur le lieu où résidait le Saint-Esprit, mais le Massachusetts le jugea sévèrement. On lui retira ses armes. Avec sa famille et plusieurs de ses partisans les plus courageux, il quitta Boston par mer et mit le cap au nord vers Great Bay. Il passa très probablement en chemin devant deux avant-postes plus anciens du New Hampshire. Le premier, à l’embouchure de la Pascataqua, s’appelait alors Strawbery Banke (aujourd’hui Portsmouth). Le second était la colonie de Dover.
