### 1. What makes Monkey D Luffy a particularly good emblem for gen Z's fights today? Explain. (1 point)

Monkey D Luffy is a good emblem because the very plot of *One Piece* is a story of rebellion against an all-powerful authority: for over a thousand episodes, his crew has been fighting the « World Government ». For young protesters who denounce corrupt or brutal rulers of their own, flying his flag casts them as the heroes of a similar fight. The symbol is also global: *One Piece* is the bestselling manga of all time, so the flag is instantly recognisable from Morocco to Madagascar and needs no translation. **In short, Luffy's flag turns a pop-culture icon into a universal banner of defiance against illegitimate power.**

### 2. Why were Indonesian officials alarmed by the use of the Luffy crew's flag? What does this reveal about how governments view symbolic protests? (1 point)

Indonesian officials were alarmed because the pirate flag was flown that summer as a direct riposte to the president, who had urged citizens to fly the national standard; waving Luffy's banner instead was an open challenge to the state, and the authorities even threatened to jail those who used it. **What this reveals is that governments treat symbolic protests as genuine political threats: a shared emblem can unite and mobilise crowds, so states try to suppress it even when it looks playful.**

### 3. How do the grievances in Madagascar, Morocco, and Peru differ? What unites these movements despite the differences? (2 points)

Madagascan protesters object to unreliable electricity and water supplies; Moroccans want better schools and medical services; Peruvians are concerned about insecurity. Despite these different triggers, the movements share a young identity, informal organisation and symbols borrowed across borders. They also express broader frustration with unequal opportunities, dishonest rulers and police violence, in a context of economic and environmental insecurity.

### 4. Explain the meaning of the following sentence: « Alaa Abd el-Fattah expressed concern, in 2019, that online culture had seen the degeneration of discussion and debate » (paragraph 5). (1 point)

He worries that digital exchanges have become less thoughtful: people react with pictures or affectionate symbols instead of developing and challenging arguments. A message may travel very quickly without encouraging serious political discussion. Online networks can therefore connect protesters while also narrowing the conversation they have with one another.

### 5. What do The Guardian mean when they write: « Protests have always been contagious » (paragraph 6)? (1 point)

When *The Guardian* writes that « protests have always been contagious », it uses a medical metaphor: just as a disease passes from one body to another, a revolt spreads from one country or one era to the next as ideas, methods and symbols are imitated. The historical examples — Europe in 1848, the Arab spring of 2011, the Black Lives Matter movement of 2020 — show that this contagion is nothing new. **Contagious here means that protest travels across borders and generations through imitation.**

### 6. « In the light of recent events, do you believe in gen Z's ability to provoke global social and political change? Why or why not? »

Can gen Z truly reshape the world, or are its viral symbols mere noise? In the light of recent events, I believe this generation has a real, though still unproven, ability to provoke global social and political change.

The first reason is the unprecedented speed at which its symbols travel. As the Luffy flag shows, a single image can spread from Morocco to Madagascar, from Indonesia to Nepal in a matter of weeks, turning local grievances into a shared, global statement. Where past movements relied on parties and unions, leaderless crowds now coordinate across borders almost instantly — and governments have taken notice: Nepal's parliament burned with the pirate flag attached to its railings.

Second, gen Z mobilises on the issues that will define this century: climate change, inequality, the cost of living, corruption. Fridays for Future, launched by a single Swedish teenager in 2018, forced climate onto the agenda of governments that had long ignored it, and youth-led protests have already helped topple governments, as in Sri Lanka in 2022. In each case, the trigger was local, but the symbol and the anger were global.

Yet this power has limits. Symbols can be repressed, as Thailand's ban on the three-finger salute or Indonesia's threats of jail show. Leaderless movements often lack the organisation needed to turn street energy into lasting reform. And online activism can degenerate into what Alaa Abd el-Fattah called circles of people sending gifs and heart emojis. Change sparked is not change built.

In conclusion, gen Z undeniably has leverage — global networks, moral urgency, cultural creativity — and it has already shaken governments. Whether it can provoke deep, lasting change will depend on its ability to move beyond viral symbols towards patient, organised political action. That, in the end, is the real test of this generation.
