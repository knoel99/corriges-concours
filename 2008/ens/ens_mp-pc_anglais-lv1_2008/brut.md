## 1. What can explain the increase in health expenditure in the US?

The editorial identifies enthusiasm for medical technology as a major source of rising expenditure. A new scanner may be purchased because doctors and patients expect clearer pictures to improve treatment, even when nobody has shown that the extra detail changes the diagnosis. Manufacturers benefit from the purchase, and a hospital may fear appearing less advanced than its neighbours if it waits. Some innovations have brought genuine improvements, so the problem is deciding which additional costs buy better outcomes. The article attributes up to half of spending growth to technology, leaving room for other explanations. For instance, a population with more patients requiring long-term care can need more appointments even at unchanged prices. Counting the uninsured measures a serious problem of access, but tells us little by itself about why spending per patient rises.

## 2. How can we evaluate the efficiency of expenditure on scientific research?

First specify what the research is meant to achieve and what would probably happen without it. For a trial of a diagnostic test, evaluators could compare the cost of research and implementation with improvements in patients' outcomes, including the consequences of unnecessary treatment. Publication of reliable negative results also has value if it prevents hospitals buying ineffective equipment. For basic research the appropriate horizon is longer. An independent review can examine whether methods are sound, results can be reproduced and data have allowed other teams to answer new questions. Counting papers alone would reward repeated publication of minor findings. I would therefore compare a portfolio of projects over time, combine measurable outcomes with expert scrutiny and explain which benefits remain uncertain. A fair assessment should allow promising work to fail while identifying programmes that repeatedly consume resources without generating usable knowledge.
