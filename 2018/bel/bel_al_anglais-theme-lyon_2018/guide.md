## Préparer le thème

**Entendre l’entrée avant de la traduire.** Le narrateur commence seul, puis entre avec les autres. Les rythmes impriment leur ardeur aux corps, mais les convives composent une mine lasse. *Would hand over*, *we would enter* et *we would disguise* installent le rite. Dans *I would not think of her again*, la même forme prend une valeur de futur dans le passé. L’allusion à la grand-mère situe le changement d’identité à l’intérieur d’une soirée.

**Déplier la phrase de Balbec.** Ce qui ouvre la phrase, c’est la quantité de bière ou de champagne. Le verbe principal français ne vient qu’après la remarque sur la saveur. *As much … as* permet de garder l’attente du lecteur jusqu’à *I would consume*. Le plaisir auparavant perceptible était facile à sacrifier. Dans la salle, l’attention manque même pour goûter le porto. Les deux louis ont été économisés pendant un mois avant d’être donnés au violoniste.

**Conserver l’observateur dans l’image.** *Je remarquai* fait surgir un individu du mouvement habituel. Sa chevelure est déjà un plumage avant que soit nommé le perroquet. *One might have thought* réserve au spectateur l’impression que la course est sans but. La fin reprend *calm* pour faire entendre le passage progressif de l’agitation à l’harmonie. La note du feuillet explique *gommeuses* par « chanteuses de café-concert ».

## Traduction en anglais

I would hand over my overcoat. We would enter the restaurant dining room to the sound of some martial march played by the Romani musicians and advance between the rows of tables where the guests had already been served, as though the path to glory lay smooth before us. And when we felt the orchestra, conferring military honours upon us and granting us this triumph we had done nothing to deserve, stir our bodies to joyful ardour with its rhythms, we would disguise our excitement with grave, icy looks and a weary step, for fear of looking like those music-hall singers who come running onstage with all the martial bearing of a victorious general to sing a bawdy verse to a warlike tune.

From that moment on I was a new man, no longer my grandmother’s grandson, for I would not think of her again until I was on my way out, but for the time being a brother to the waiters who were about to serve us.

As much beer, let alone champagne, as I would have been loath to drink in a whole week at Balbec, even though in my calmer, more lucid moments I could distinctly appreciate the pleasure of tasting such drinks and readily forgo it, I would consume in the space of an hour, adding a few drops of port though I was too distracted to savour it. And to the violinist who had just finished playing I would give the two gold louis I had been saving for a month to buy something I could no longer remember. Some of the waiters, let loose among the tables, would race away at full tilt, each with a dish on his outstretched palm, and the object of this sort of race seemed to be to get to the end without dropping it. Sure enough, the chocolate soufflés arrived intact, and in spite of the gallop that must have jolted them, the boiled potatoes were still arranged around the Pauillac lamb exactly as they had been at the outset. One of the waiters caught my eye. He was very tall, with a magnificent plume of black hair, his face made up in a shade more suggestive of certain rare species of bird than of the human race. As he ran without respite and, one might have thought, without purpose from one end of the room to the other, he put one in mind of one of those macaws whose fiery colours and inexplicable restlessness fill the great aviaries of zoological gardens. Soon the spectacle resolved itself, to my eyes at least, into a pattern at once nobler and calmer. All that dizzying activity settled into a calm harmony.
