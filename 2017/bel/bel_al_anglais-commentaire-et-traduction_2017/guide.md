Commentaire en anglais, p. 4–5, l. 1–57, des vestiges de l’abbaye aux deux hommes dans le cimetière. Version française, p. 4, l. 1–18, du début « Over a space » à « with a rank vegetation. ». La coupure imprimée après les fenêtres appartient au sujet. Le bétail entre dans la phrase suivante.

Le passage de Sybil, or The Two Nations (1845), de Benjamin Disraeli, se déroule en 1837. La notice rappelle les incendies de meules et l’origine des biens de la famille Marney. La version porte sur les vestiges de l’abbaye jusqu’à la phrase sur la végétation qui recouvre la nef.

## Préparer l’épreuve

Faire d’abord le trajet sans Egremont. Les bâtiments sont regardés de la périphérie vers le centre avant que le personnage prenne le relais. Souligner les indications d’échelle, l’ancienne destination de l’hospice et les mots qui font de l’aide un droit. Puis suivre le déplacement d’Egremont du bétail aux cellules et de la position élevée aux hommes qui parlent. La description préparait une enquête sociale.

Dans la version, distinguer les bâtiments de service (« offices »), l’hospice et l’église abbatiale. Le ciel d’été forme désormais la voûte. Rank qualifie ici une végétation exubérante, dense et envahissante. Ne pas anticiper l’arrivée du bétail, qui suit la dernière phrase à traduire. Dans le commentaire, ne pas tenir la dernière question d’Egremont pour la preuve que tous les paysans du passé vivaient dans l’aisance.

La tournure clivée annonce un emplacement, puis retarde le nom de l’église. En français, « c’est au centre […] que se dressait » permet de garder cette attente. Les deux plus-que-parfaits inscrivent la résistance de la pierre au temps et celle de sa beauté à la colère humaine dans deux histoires parallèles.

## Commentaire

### Who can claim shelter at Marney Abbey?

The first observer of Marney Abbey has no name. Someone can still trace what was once a house or a garden, someone else might take the same walk and verify those remains. It is some time before Charles Egremont appears to exercise his quite personal judgement about the cows lying in the nave. And yet nobody approaches these ruins on entirely equal terms. As the introduction to the passage tells us, the estate of his brother, Lord Marney, stands on former Church property. How does Disraeli turn an apparently available pleasure, the contemplation of an old abbey on a summer day, into a question about the distribution of the benefits of England's history?

The opening invitation is remarkably exact about space and increasingly generous about time. Ten acres set a lower limit to the extent of the remains, two acres to the church at the centre. But to see something on this scale one must also read what survives. A mossy fragment tells of a building which no longer stands. A difference in the materials and proportions makes the hospital easier to trace than the abbot's dwelling. The description then invites us to read an English word as carefully as a ruin. A modern reader would expect sickness in a hospital. The narrator restores travellers, pilgrims and peasants to an earlier meaning of hospitality. This lexical shift has a political consequence. When the needy come to the gate they can claim food and clothes. A form of provision which had the force of a right might disappear from the language even before the last stones of the building had disappeared from the earth.

There is undeniably an element of idealisation in this account. A proud baron and a solitary pilgrim are made into the extremes of a strikingly inclusive series. The narrator knows of no refusal at the gate. What a ruined wall can show us about its construction is rather different from what it can prove about an institution's unfailing generosity. By joining the two in one sustained sentence, the description borrows the authority of the visible for its historical reconstruction. This is part of its eloquence. It is also a reason to attend closely to the questions the novel will ask, rather than taking an answer on trust. What the past affords is at least a standard by which to ask what rights attach to a great estate in the present.

At the centre of the ruin, the sentence itself becomes architectural. Conditions, measurements and concessions are in place before the delayed identification of the Abbey church. Summer sky has taken the place of its vault. The loss makes possible a new visual pleasure, just as the absence of the window glass allows the great arch and its remaining tracery to stand out. For Egremont, this accommodation between nature and Christian art meets its limit with the cattle. He sees profanation where the animals find relief from the heat. The narrator has quietly specified that they need shelter, the very thing which travellers used to demand from the abbey. Egremont turns away with a sigh. He has recognized an offence against beauty more readily than he has recognized a continuity of need. The point is not to equate the cattle with the poor, but to notice what his first response singles out.

The garden offers one inconvenient witness to the abbey’s claim to have mastered time. Its solitary yew is older than the masonry, or so tradition says. The qualification distinguishes what the visitor actually sees from the authority on which any story about its age depends. The path then takes Egremont up an imperfect staircase. We now learn that he knows the way. A visitor might well have hesitated at the ascent he negotiates with ease. The family connection that gives him such access to the ruins will make the view from above uncomfortable. First, however, the writing gives both character and reader the familiar freedoms of a Romantic scene. The weary wind has found somewhere to sleep, the river drifts half conscious, and even the shafts of sunlight seem to bask. The description lends to nature a holiday which the novel is about to ask who can afford. Motion is suspended outside the observer. Inside his mind something *stirs*. The almost noiseless transition matters. What seemed the perfection of solitude makes two earlier voices audible again, those of the farmer and the farm steward.

Egremont’s first question is personal. Has England ceased to be the place it was in his light-hearted youth? An adult looking back might easily mistake the loss of his own ease for a national transformation. The next question introduces the poor, who cannot be presumed to have shared that youth. Changes of faith and dynasty now give way to changes in property and the location of power. The word *ruins* becomes literal and political at once. His house stands among the remains of a religious body. His order rose upon its displacement, and might in turn be displaced. The grammar will no longer allow an idle spectator. The repeated possessive identifying his house and his order places the observer inside the sequence he would like to survey.

Nor does the scale stop at the family. Who made the national wealth whose concentration has enabled a limited class to speak as if it were the nation? The swelling sequence of superlatives reaches religion by way of power, freedom and moral superiority. Coming after the picture of the Poor's Portal, this last boast has a particularly sharp edge. An established test of religious greatness has been made concrete in the first paragraph. What is now available to a hungry worker? At this point character and narrator come very close. The series of rhetorical questions has the reach of a public speech; its first occasion, however, was the discomfort of a young man whose relation to the estate will not let him off the hook.

The comparison most likely to disturb him comes in the family name. Why would the ricks of the earls be burnt and those of the abbots spared? The place name survives the transfer of property and makes the difference in the possessors visible. Egremont does not know, simply by looking at the ruins, what every peasant under the abbots would have done. He has learned enough to put a question to his own class. The final interruption also unsettles the grammar. The opening participle of the last sentence belongs in sense to the brooding Egremont, yet the grammatical subject that follows is the voices which break in on him. His meditation loses its hold even on the sentence. Two voices reach him from the cemetery, where one man is examining a tomb. What began as a study in solitary vision ends before other people who may read the past differently. Disraeli has brought the privileged reader of the landscape far enough to need interlocutors.

## Version française

Sur une étendue d’au moins dix acres se voyaient encore les vestiges de la grande abbaye. Aux limites du site, c’étaient pour la plupart des restes croulants, couverts de mousse, qui indiquaient où s’élevaient jadis les bâtiments de service et où s’étageaient les jardins en terrasses des anciens propriétaires. Ici se dessinait encore le logis du seigneur abbé, et là, plus nettement encore, parce qu’on l’avait conçu plus vaste et bâti de matériaux destinés à durer plus longtemps encore, le grand hospice. Ce nom ne désignait pas alors le séjour de la maladie, mais un lieu où l’on s’acquittait de tous les devoirs de l’hospitalité. Le voyageur, de l’orgueilleux baron au pèlerin solitaire, y demandait un abri et un secours qui ne lui étaient jamais refusés. À sa porte, appelée le Portail des pauvres, les paysans des terres de l’abbaye pouvaient, s’ils étaient dans le besoin, venir matin et soir réclamer de quoi se vêtir et se nourrir.

Mais c’est au centre de ce champ de ruines, sur une étendue d’au moins deux acres, que se dressait encore, avec une solidité qui avait défié le temps et une beauté qui avait fini par détourner la colère des hommes, dans une forme et un état sinon parfaits, du moins admirables, l’une des plus nobles réalisations de l’art chrétien, l’église abbatiale. Elle n’avait plus pour toit que la voûte de ce ciel d’été. De ses fenêtres somptueuses il ne demeurait que la symétrie de leurs vastes arcs et quelques vestiges entrelacés de leur extravagant réseau de pierre, mais le reste était intact. […]

La nef de l’église était en maint endroit envahie par les ronces, et partout couverte d’une végétation foisonnante.
