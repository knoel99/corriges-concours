**Can the Electricity Bill Pay a Climate Dividend?**

A computer learning to process language and a wind farm predicting tomorrow's output appear to belong on opposite sides of a climate balance sheet. Both use machine learning. In New Scientist, Donna Lu reports on the electricity required to develop large language models. A Forbes graphic translates a particularly demanding estimate into familiar comparisons. Thomas Griffin in Forbes and Angeli Mehta for Reuters Events examine the infrastructure behind such services and the savings they might enable. Assessing their environmental contribution requires specifying the application, its source of electricity and the activity it can actually displace.

The apparent weightlessness of a digital service conceals material work. Lu describes Massachusetts researchers measuring a day's energy consumption for each of four language models and extrapolating over the training times reported by their developers. Repeatedly trying out different designs could increase the computing time dramatically. Using average American electricity emissions, the researchers estimated a footprint as high as 284 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent. In the Forbes chart, training the model corresponds to 626.2 thousand pounds, compared with 126 thousand for manufacturing and fuelling an average American car over its lifetime. The roughly fivefold difference describes one demanding training configuration. It cannot be applied to each translation subsequently requested by a user.

Griffin places this cost within a larger industrial system. Machines generate heat as they process data, so keeping a facility operational requires additional energy for cooling. More efficient equipment may be overwhelmed by the volume of services consumers want. Lu adds that a national average electricity mix cannot capture differences between individual providers, some of which buy renewable power under long contracts. The choice of algorithm, the efficiency of a data centre and its electricity supply therefore offer separate opportunities to reduce emissions. A supplier improving one measure can still face rising total consumption as demand expands.

Customers also exert pressure in the opposite direction. Griffin reports that businesses buying cloud services increasingly want suppliers to account for their environmental performance. Providers are investing in wind or solar power, measuring the footprint of customer deployments and reconsidering cooling methods. Mehta gives a further twist to the story. A Californian firm proposes using AI itself to eliminate unnecessary cooling in data centres. Its announced emissions cuts are targets, whose achievement depends on deployment with partners. They cannot be entered as realised savings alongside the researchers' training estimate.

Elsewhere the potential benefits depend on information becoming available in time to act. Mehta describes a British company identifying individual appliances' consumption from household electricity readings, giving residents more specific guidance than a single total. At a larger scale, forecasts of output can help grid operators accommodate variable renewable supplies. Google reports that advance forecasts increased the value of its wind energy by twenty per cent. A predictable delivery can be sold on better terms even when the wind supplies the same amount of electricity. Such applications connect British and American efforts to make renewable power competitive, while also raising the question of what would induce households to share their data.

The documents consequently describe a conditional contribution to climate policy. The car comparison makes the magnitude of one largely invisible cost intelligible, while the cooling and grid examples identify places where a corresponding gain might be measured. Neither figure can stand in for a full balance sheet. The training method, power supply, scale of adoption and behaviour of users determine the expense. The energy that a better forecast or control system saves determines the return. Only by considering both in the same setting can an apparently smart service be judged environmentally effective over the life of the equipment that supports it.

## B. Texte d’opinion

**Réagir au Guardian.** Le journal rapproche deux difficultés, l’opacité de certaines décisions et la diffusion d’outils puissants dont l’entraînement a déjà été payé. AlphaZero sait jouer à des jeux à information parfaite. La comparaison avec un dossier médical offre un contre-exemple où les données restent incomplètes et les objectifs discutables. La position défendue ci-dessous donne à l’institution utilisatrice une obligation de justification et de recours.

**A Patient Is Entitled to More Than a Winning Move**

The Guardian is right to be impressed by AlphaZero and right to be uneasy about it. A player can accept an extraordinary chess move without understanding it. The result will eventually appear on the board, the rules were agreed in advance and a rematch is possible. If a hospital sends somebody home after a computer has assessed a scan, none of those conditions holds. I would put responsibility for that difference squarely on the institution that decides to use the system.

One of the editorial's best observations concerns the limits of perfect information. A grandmaster and a computer can see every piece. In medicine a missing entry might mean a symptom was absent, nobody asked about it or the patient could find no words to describe it. A machine trained on the record cannot turn those possibilities into a certainty by calculating faster. Nor can it decide what risk a particular patient would accept to avoid a painful procedure. The ambition to win a game has one remarkably clear objective. A patient's aims deserve a conversation.

I am less persuaded that an inability to reconstruct every step of a machine's reasoning should itself disqualify a system. Experienced doctors sometimes recognise a worrying pattern before they can articulate the clues. We trust them conditionally, because their performance can be tested, they can request another opinion and a professional body can investigate a mistake. I would demand comparable safeguards for software, together with evidence about the populations in which it was tested. A neat list of factors generated after the event is of limited use if it would tell the same story when the machine makes a serious error.

The Guardian calls the wider availability of trained systems a move towards anarchy. Its concern about the concentration of power in irresponsible hands is justified. Distribution can also enable a small hospital to obtain assistance previously available only at a wealthy research centre. Keeping every useful system under the exclusive control of its inventor might turn a safety argument into a defence of monopoly. Access to a useful prediction and authority to act on it should be treated separately. A clinic might welcome a second reading of a scan while requiring a qualified professional to authorise a consequential decision.

Who pays when that arrangement fails? I would ask the hospital to name the person who can reopen a case, record disagreements with the software and explain to patients how to obtain another review. The supplier should provide the information needed to investigate failures. A hospital board should be able to withdraw the tool if evidence changes. These are duties an elected government or professional regulator can enforce even when researchers still disagree about how a neural network represents a pattern.

A patient leaving the consultation deserves an answer to three ordinary questions. What did you find, how sure are you and whom can I speak to if the symptoms continue? The Guardian fears that a new form of power may take understanding away from us. Holding the people who deploy it answerable for those three questions would put some of that power back in the patient's hands.
