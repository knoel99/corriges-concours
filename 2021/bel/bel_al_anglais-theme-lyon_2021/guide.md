## Préparer le thème

**Localiser le mouvement.** À la fin de la saison des pluies, les fils reviennent chaque soir de la périphérie vers le centre-ville. Le sujet de *avaient fui* est la génération des parents. *Would fill up* exprime le retour périodique et *had fled* la fuite antérieure. Les deux listes de noms distinguent les lieux d’origine et les familles propriétaires.

**Maintenir l’inventaire.** Les parents ont échangé leurs maisons pour des villas, puis transformé leurs demeures familiales en commerces. Les fausses ardoises et les fausses mansardes font deux fois entendre l’imitation. Les trois formes des piscines doivent rester distinctes. Le contrôle matériel de la ville explique la singularité du centre de recherche introduit au quatrième paragraphe.

**Passer du portrait public à la rencontre.** Thomas descend d’une lignée de lettrés qui a fourni des serviteurs à l’État tout en gardant ses distances à l’égard du pouvoir. La première personne n’arrive qu’au dernier paragraphe. Retrouver alors la répétition de *old-fashioned*, la guayabera et les petits souliers noirs. La dernière phrase place le nom imposant du lieu d’étude en regard du sens concret et modeste donné à *Emporio*, ici *the Market Hall*.

## Traduction en anglais

At the end of the rainy season the Valley would fill up every evening. Behind tinted windows, safe inside gleaming vehicles painted with flames, dragons, ninjas and Aztec warriors, the sons of the prominent families reclaimed the centre of town, which their parents had fled on account of its unhealthy conditions. They came in from the outskirts, from the ranches and the housing estates of the rich, from Glorieta, Media Luna, Porvenir, Huertas and Nuevo Mundo. Billionaires, heirs to the strawberry empire, the Escalantes, the Chamorros, the Patricios, the De la Vegas, the De la Vergnes, the Olguins, the Olids, the Olmoses …

Long ago their parents had traded their grand, ramshackle old houses of pink stone for Californian-style concrete villas painted red and yellow, neo-Gothic castles with imitation slate roofs topped with imitation dormers, porches ringed with marble columns and rooms full of hot tubs, swimming pools shaped like hearts, guitars and strawberries.

But they had never relinquished their claim to the town. They had turned their family homes into shopping arcades, multi-storey car parks, cinemas, ice-cream parlours or restaurants serving steaks grilled gaucho-style.

In the midst of this ruined town, these potholed roads, these open sewers, Don Thomas had founded the Emporio, a centre for research and higher education devoted to the humanities and the pursuit of knowledge.

Thomas Moises did not come from any of the prominent families of strawberry growers and avocado producers who held the whole Valley in their hands. He was the last scion of a long line of scholars and local dignitaries who had supplied the state with judges, schoolteachers and parish priests, and had managed to live through wars and revolutions while keeping their distance from power. He was not a native of the Valley but came from Quitupan, a mountain village at the headwaters of the Tepalcatepec River.

The first time I met him, in his office at the Emporio, he received me with a kindly reserve that I found appealing. I saw a plump little man with olive skin, very black hair, the gentle eyes of an Indian and an old-fashioned brush moustache. Indeed, everything about him was old-fashioned. He wore a brown suit whose jacket looked rather worn, a blue guayabera shirt and, on his small feet, immaculately polished black shoes. At fifty, after a lifetime spent teaching history at universities, he had founded this little college out of love for his native region, in the hope of saving what could still be saved of its traditions and its memory. To this Athenaeum he had given the modest name of Emporio, meaning the Market Hall.
