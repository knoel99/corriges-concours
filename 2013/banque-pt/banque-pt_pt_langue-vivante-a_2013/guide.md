### Proposition rédigée

**A gas dividend with conditions attached**

In a field near Blackpool, a child holds a sign asking what shale gas will mean for the future. On the other side of the Atlantic, supporters of the industry ask what declining manufacturing towns can hope for today. The distance between those two questions helps explain how the same discovery can suggest both opportunity and loss. Recoverable reserves promise an additional source of energy, but the benefits depend on what the gas replaces and on who bears the local costs.

In the United States, shale rose from one per cent of natural gas supplies in 2000 to thirty per cent at the time of David Brooks’s article. Cheaper gas lowered costs for consumers and encouraged manufacturers to invest at home. Steel tubes ordered for the industry, for example, meant new industrial activity in Ohio. Regions hit by unemployment could expect jobs and tax revenue, while greater domestic supply reduced dependence on foreign producers. The international chart gives some measure of the wider opportunity. Russia leads in total recoverable natural gas, but the bars distinguish conventional gas from shale and other sources. China also holds sizeable shale resources. Estimates of what can be recovered do not tell us when a country will produce it or on what terms.

The appeal extends beyond the price of electricity. Where gas replaces coal, burning it releases roughly half as much carbon dioxide. For The Economist, it could therefore buy time for cleaner technologies to mature. Yet a price advantage over coal does not make gas an adequate response to climate change. Methane released before the gas reaches a power station could erode the benefit. Nor does a saving on energy automatically become an investment in renewables. The article sees a role for a carbon price in steering those decisions.

The Guardian looks at a different balance sheet. Residents worry about drinking water, the handling of wastewater and possible earth tremors. Uncertainty about the composition and destination of waste makes independent assessment difficult. A community asked to accept these risks may have little share in the gains enjoyed by distant consumers. The child photographed at the British protest gives that concern a time dimension. A new job can benefit a family immediately, whereas some effects on its surroundings may only become clear much later.

The disagreement concerns the conditions under which the promise could be kept. The American experience provides evidence of lower prices and new investment, while the chart suggests that the opportunity extends beyond one country. Neither settles the question raised in the field near Blackpool. Public access to information, protection of water and attention to emissions throughout production all affect whether an economic gain also counts as an environmental improvement. A bridge to a cleaner energy supply is only as valuable as the destination that investment actually reaches.
