## 1. What place does compassion occupy in politics today, according to the author?

Associer l’usage rhétorique de la compassion, son apparition dans l’action collective et son enracinement dans la perception démocratique d’autrui. Les références à Tocqueville et Rousseau expliquent pourquoi les seules évolutions économiques récentes ne suffisent pas.

Compassion has become a common language across political divisions. Leaders address people as victims of suffering, while campaigners draw attention to hardship in order to change public policy. Social problems once described as conflicts between classes are increasingly discussed in terms of insecurity, exclusion and protection. The decline of political movements centred on workers' struggles helps explain this change, as do unemployment and insecure work since the end of the postwar boom. Yet Revault d’Allonnes sees deeper roots than a change in economic circumstances. Following Tocqueville, she links the democratic equalisation of conditions to people's growing readiness to recognise others as beings like themselves. Behind that account stands Rousseau's understanding of pity as a starting point for social attachment. Compassion therefore shapes how political problems are perceived as well as how politicians appeal to voters.

## 2. Is it possible or desirable to govern without compassion?

Traiter séparément la possibilité et la valeur d’un gouvernement sans compassion. Un exemple de secours permet ensuite de mettre à l’épreuve le conflit entre émotion immédiate et égalité de traitement.

A government can issue orders, collect taxes and enforce rules without feeling much concern for the people affected. History offers plenty of examples. I doubt, however, that such a government can reliably discover what its rules actually do. An official who listens to families displaced by a flood may learn that the documents required to obtain relief were themselves destroyed in the water. Compassion makes an administrative obstacle visible and gives the official a reason to remove it.

Yet sympathy alone is a poor way to distribute the relief budget. A family interviewed on television may attract generous support while a quieter neighbour with the same needs receives nothing. A fair response requires published criteria, officials empowered to examine unusual cases and a means of appealing a refusal. Those safeguards can protect people whom no minister has ever met.

I would therefore distinguish a willingness to attend to suffering from the public performance of emotion. Citizens are entitled to question a leader who sheds tears before cameras and leaves a damaging rule intact. They have better reason to trust an institution which listens carefully, changes the rule and checks that assistance reaches people out of sight.
