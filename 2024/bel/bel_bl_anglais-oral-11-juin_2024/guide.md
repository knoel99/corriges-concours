## Using the booklet

Each extract below is a separate possible examination text. The common oral gives you 90 minutes to prepare one document, followed by a 20-minute presentation in English and 10 minutes of questions. The jury expects a thematic commentary grounded in passages from the document. You can introduce a short phrase while discussing it without beginning by reading an extract aloud.

In preparation, mark the speaker, addressee, date and occasion. Choose three short passages from different parts of the document. For each, identify a linguistic choice, its immediate effect and one historical detail that changes how you read it. Reserve time to rehearse the transition between the three claims and the answer to the opening question.

## 1. James Madison

*Federalist No. 51* · 1788 · page 1 of the official booklet.

### Preparing the argument

> [!INDICATION]
> Sketch two divisions on different axes. Add a third heading for the danger a majority poses to a minority. Find the change of subject at the end of line 28 before choosing the order of your presentation.

**Language in context.** A “negative” in this passage is a power to veto. “Compound” describes the combination of levels of government, while “department” here means a branch of public authority.

### Opening

Why would a government that derives its authority from the people need protection against a majority of those people? James Madison confronts that difficulty in Federalist No. 51, published in 1788 as part of the campaign for ratification of the proposed US Constitution. He addresses readers who fear the concentration of authority and readers who doubt that a large republic can work. The argument starts from a modest assessment of human motives. It then builds a compound system of government and, finally, asks society itself to supply a further safeguard.

### Making private incentives serve public purposes

The opening sentence grants that dependence on the electorate is the first control on government. The qualification that follows is decisive. Experience shows that elections alone cannot govern every decision made between them. Madison's “auxiliary precautions” are therefore compatible with popular sovereignty. They provide an institutional answer to the problem of time: an official is always in office, whereas a citizen votes only periodically.

The figure of the sentinel in lines 4–7 reverses an ordinary moral expectation. Personal interest, often thought an enemy of public virtue, can guard a public right when offices are designed so that one official has reason to resist another's encroachment. The argument depends on that design. Competition left entirely to itself is no guarantee of justice. By speaking in terms of circumstances and incentives, Madison makes the Constitution credible even to a reader who expects officials to behave less than ideally.

### Why separation sometimes requires inequality

Madison does not imagine three identical institutions with interchangeable powers. He believes the legislature naturally predominates in a republic. Dividing it into branches chosen differently can keep that predominance from hardening into unchecked authority. The same passage considers strengthening the comparatively weak executive. Yet Madison hesitates over an absolute power to block legislation. His provisional language is part of the argument: one must consider whether a protection is safe and whether it will be sufficient on its own.

Lines 20–28 then distinguish two levels of division. In a federal republic, authority first passes to separate national and state governments. Within a government it is divided again among departments. Geographical division and functional division answer different questions about the exercise of the same popular authority. Calling the result a double protection lets the advocate turn the complexity of the proposed system, a potential objection to ratification, into a reason to support it.

### The people can threaten one another

The second danger in the final paragraph is horizontal. Even if rulers obey the electorate, one group of citizens may use a majority to impose an injustice on another. An independent, hereditary authority might frustrate such a majority, but it would have interests of its own and lack a secure democratic basis. Madison prefers a society containing many interests, in which forming a consistently oppressive coalition should be harder.

His claim is a matter of probability. The final qualifiers promise increased difficulty, not the impossibility of injustice. The reader may also ask who counted as a political participant in 1788. Enslaved people and many others were excluded from effective political power. That gap matters analytically. The author describes a mechanism for protecting plural interests, but the social and legal definition of membership determines which interests the mechanism can represent.

![Madison’s two institutional divisions and his additional social answer to majority domination.](figures/reading-the-evidence.svg)

### Conclusion

Madison's republic relies on citizens at the source of authority, officeholders who check one another and a society sufficiently diverse to frustrate an unjust combination. Its strongest claim is that these devices can work together without placing a hereditary guardian above the people. Its limit is that neither institutional division nor social diversity guarantees meaningful political membership.

### Questions from the jury

**Does the text assume that citizens always want the same thing?**

The last paragraph relies on precisely the opposite expectation. Distinct interests are a source of conflict but can also prevent a stable majority from dominating everyone else.

**Why retain the words suggesting uncertainty at the end?**

They keep the conclusion commensurate with the argument. Pluralism changes the conditions for combination. It cannot supply a mathematical guarantee of justice or substitute for political participation.

## 2. George W. Bush

*President Bush’s address in Atlanta, Ga.* · 8 November 2001 · pages 2–3 of the official booklet.

### Preparing the argument

> [!INDICATION]
> Trace the changing meaning of “responsibility” from government to citizens. Mark the change from mourning, to institutional evidence, to a future whose duration the president cannot specify.

**Language in context.** “Homeland” brings the intimacy of home into the vocabulary of national government. Distinguish the office named in 2001 from the department established by later legislation.

### Opening

In Atlanta on 8 November 2001, George W. Bush had to explain what an extraordinary national crisis would require of people going about their ordinary lives. The address follows the attacks of 11 September and the beginning of US military action in Afghanistan. Its audience includes Americans far beyond Atlanta. The question is how the president converts a common experience of loss into consent for a lasting change in the relationship between the federal government and the public.

### Creating a collective subject out of different experiences

The opening catalogues funerals, fear and uncertainty. The repeated first-person plural gives these experiences a single political voice. Bush then selects 10 September as the boundary between an old and a new nation. This choice makes a single day the organising principle of a longer story: innocence has given way to suffering and, in his telling, greater resolve. Two adjectives with opposite emotional associations, sadness and strength, can now describe the same people.

The portrait of the enemy further consolidates that collective identity. The speech attributes to the enemy a hatred reaching across religious and national categories, while American society is identified with respect for life and freedom. It is important to keep the status of these statements clear: they are the president's moral framing of an adversary. They give grief a public meaning, but also narrow the space in which a listener might ask whether different responses could uphold the same values.

### Naming a new field of government

The middle of the extract moves from the scale of civilisation to the names of officials and institutions. Tom Ridge is introduced through his previous office and Bush's personal confidence in him. The newly created Office of Homeland Security can thus appear both novel enough to meet the crisis and reassuringly familiar. The speaker also connects federal coordination with state and local governments and private organisations. This is a political redefinition of domestic security as a responsibility that crosses ordinary institutional boundaries.

The reference to new federal legislation is likewise presented as evidence that protection has improved. Its provisions are described through their purpose, leaving questions about oversight and rights largely unexamined in this passage. In November 2001 the body Bush names was an office. The cabinet-level Department of Homeland Security came later. Preserving the distinction lets us hear the speech as an effort to make institutional change credible while it was still unfolding.

### Asking citizens both to carry on and to change

The discussion of public alerts contains a revealing tension. Americans are encouraged to continue normal life and to cultivate a new attentiveness. The first injunction promises reassurance. The second makes participation in protection part of everyday citizenship. A listener can therefore be told that the government is in control and that security also depends on the listener's conduct.

The close connects obligations at home with the administration's international policy. Terms drawn from military life migrate into descriptions of national purpose. The certainty of the ending contrasts with the admission on the final page that the future course of events cannot be known. By separating uncertainty about circumstances from confidence about ultimate purpose, Bush can ask for endurance without promising a short timetable. That distinction is one reason the brief continuation on page 3 belongs to the argument.

### Conclusion

The address makes September's losses the basis for a continuing compact. Government is to coordinate more extensively, and citizens are to act with resolve while sustaining ordinary social life. The speech powerfully articulates a need for reassurance. Its omissions also show how a moment of emergency can make large questions about public authority seem already settled.

### Questions from the jury

**Does describing the speech's persuasive function imply that public fear was invented?**

No. The losses and fear are the conditions to which the speech responds. The political choice lies in which obligations the president asks listeners to draw from those experiences.

**Who is included in the final first-person plural?**

It directly invites citizens into a shared purpose and speaks beyond an audience physically present in Georgia. It also tends to absorb disagreement about the means into a common assertion about national resolve.

## 3. George III

*Letter written in the 1780s (precise year unknown)* · 1780s · page 4 of the official booklet.

### Preparing the argument

> [!INDICATION]
> Use the opening punctuation to establish the letter's exploratory voice. Make three columns for a producer, a competitor and a trading partner. Track how the former colonies move from one category to another.

**Language in context.** “Tenure” concerns how securely a possession is held. The king applies the language of property and risk to faraway territories, which helps explain the change of tone from lament to calculation.

### Opening

George III's letter opens like a lament and quickly becomes an audit. The jury dates it to the 1780s and gives no precise year. The writer considers the consequences for Britain of losing the American colonies and asks what policy should follow. A useful question is how a sovereign can acknowledge a political defeat while revising the very measure by which that defeat is judged. His successive measures are the cost of colonial possession, the potential of independent trade and the vulnerability of Britain's remaining empire.

### A king conducts an argument with inherited policy

The opening exclamation gives the loss a finality that the next questions immediately disturb. Must Britain fall with the blow, and where might a remedy be found? We can hear a writer trying out different answers rather than announcing a completed public programme. The extract identifies the writer and the decade. It gives no named recipient, so the shifts of tone should not be converted into a claim about a specific private conversation.

Invoking a century of colonial policy makes revision seem a response to a genuinely new situation. The description of Americans as free, industrious and prosperous concedes much to the former colonies. Yet the king follows the chain of adjectives to the market they created for British manufacturers. His focus remains the consequences for the British polity. Praise of the colonists’ energies can coexist with a strongly metropolitan measure of their importance.

### Keeping different colonial accounts

In the central passage George separates the northern colonies from tobacco, rice and sugar producers. The north competed with Britain in shipping and fishing. Other possessions supplied revenues he presents as much easier to identify. He sets these earnings against the costs of successive wars. The apparent precision of the running account helps challenge the assumption that an increase in territory always increases the wealth of the imperial centre.

Read the figures as elements of that argument. They do not constitute a complete balance sheet, and the categories group whole societies according to what their economic activities yield to Britain. In particular, a valuation of sugar and rice through metropolitan returns leaves enslaved labour largely outside the frame. This observation does not require assigning an unexpressed position to the king. It follows from noticing who counts as a cost, who appears as a competitor and whose work appears only as a commodity.

### Reimagining connection without abandoning empire

The letter makes its sharpest turn in lines 22–28. Trading with the Americans as friends might bring more advantage than governing them as subjects. The claim that economic benefit could persist even during rebellion unsettles the rationale for enforcing political obedience. The loss of sovereignty need not entail the loss of a market. In this respect a letter about an imperial failure is also an attempt to imagine post-independence relations.

The closing passage limits how far the revision goes. The East and West Indies retain fundamental economic importance in the king's account. Yet their value increases the potential cost of losing them, while distance makes possession contingent on British resources. The paradox is that an economically valuable empire can generate insecurity precisely through what it puts at risk. The final preference for preserving existing territories weighs continued empire against the cost of further expansion.

### Conclusion

The text offers neither unbroken imperial confidence nor a general case for emancipation. It changes the accounting rules by which one particular loss is measured. American independence becomes an occasion for separating political control from commercial connection and for scrutinising Britain's remaining commitments on recognisably imperial terms.

### Questions from the jury

**Can we date the letter to the Treaty of Paris itself?**

The extract belongs to the aftermath of the loss of the colonies. The peace treaty of 1783 is relevant context, but the jury explicitly leaves the precise year unknown. Nor does it identify the addressee.

**Does the case for commercial friendship imply opposition to imperial government everywhere?**

The closing paragraphs value other imperial possessions intensely. The revision is selective and driven by the costs and benefits George perceives for Britain.

## 4. Harold Macmillan

*Speech before the South African Parliament* · 3 February 1960 · pages 5–6 of the official booklet.

### Preparing the argument

> [!INDICATION]
> Identify the audience before interpreting “we”. Compare the impersonal weather image on page 5 with the personal language of merit and opportunity on page 6. Explain what can be said indirectly in Parliament through that shift.

**Language in context.** “The uncommitted” refers to countries or peoples whose Cold War alignment the speaker regards as undecided. It is Macmillan's framing and does not exhaust their own political priorities.

### Opening

A British prime minister tells South Africa's Parliament that African nationalism has to be accepted as a political fact. Harold Macmillan delivered the speech on 3 February 1960, when African states were gaining independence and South Africa's government enforced apartheid. The most difficult feature of the address is its simultaneous appeal to African aspirations, a Western alliance and an audience committed to racial hierarchy. How does Macmillan try to change what that audience will accept while preserving a future for British influence?

### A metaphor that changes the terms of debate

Macmillan begins his history in Europe, with the emergence of independent nations after the Roman Empire. He then moves to Asia and Africa. The sequence argues that wanting national independence is neither an exotic demand nor a passing disturbance. His parliamentary listeners are asked to recognise in African nationalism a process that Europeans have long considered legitimate in their own history.

The wind metaphor at line 10 gives the development a momentum beyond the approval of any one government. The pronouns place the visiting speaker and his hosts in the position of people who have to decide how to respond. Yet weather has no organisers or political arguments. Metaphor can make a demand harder to dismiss while obscuring those who fought to advance it. That double effect is visible again when Macmillan credits Western education, science and communications with producing the conditions for national consciousness.

### Placing independence within the Cold War

The questions around lines 19–24 shift the horizon from African independence to global alignment. The peoples of Asia and Africa appear as potential political decision-makers, but their possible choices are organised around an East–West division. Britain can consequently present decolonisation as a means of maintaining Western influence through consent. Macmillan's catalogue of newly independent Commonwealth countries supplies examples of this possibility and points forward to Nigeria.

The grammar of responsibility remains revealing. Britain claims responsibility for its decisions to grant independence, even as the speech recognises that anti-colonial nationalism has a momentum of its own. These are different accounts of agency. Their coexistence helps Macmillan defend policy at home and acknowledge change abroad. An independent listener might hear a welcome recognition of self-government while objecting to independence being valued chiefly for its effect on the diplomatic balance.

### An argument his hosts cannot fully endorse

On page 6 Macmillan moves from national sovereignty to individual advancement. A society where merit alone determines one's prospects cannot easily be reconciled with a political order based on racial classification. The challenge to apartheid becomes especially pointed because it is expressed as a principle the audience is implicitly invited to share. The speech also represents the Commonwealth as an association in which legitimate disagreement can coexist with cooperation.

That formulation offers the hosts a dignified way to hear criticism. It leaves important matters open, among them how an association committed to individual rights should respond when one government's domestic practices contradict them. The final invitation to unity therefore carries two meanings. It is a diplomatic courtesy and a warning that an inflexible racial order might endanger the wider association that the audience is asked to value.

### Conclusion

Macmillan makes decolonisation appear historically unavoidable and potentially favourable to Britain's continuing international role. His audience explains both the force and the indirectness of the speech. Recognising African political agency, defending the Commonwealth and questioning racial hierarchy are brought together, but the tensions between them do not disappear.

### Questions from the jury

**Why begin a speech about Africa with European history?**

The comparison exposes a double standard in treating national self-government as natural in Europe and anomalous elsewhere. It also allows Macmillan to account for African history through a path familiar to his parliamentary audience.

**What distinguishes membership of the Commonwealth from continued colonial status in his argument?**

He describes an association of sovereign states free to disagree, so common purposes must be achieved through cooperation. The description is also a bid to preserve influence after formal imperial authority has receded.
